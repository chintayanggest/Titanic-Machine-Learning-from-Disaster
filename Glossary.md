# Titanic
before we write a single line of Python, we must make assumptions. In Data Science, this is called Hypothesis Generation. We use logic and "Domain Knowledge" (like knowing about the Titanic movie) to guess what matters. Then, we use VS Code and EDA (Exploratory Data Analysis) to prove if our assumptions are right or wrong!
Domain Knowledge: Background knowledge of the specific field the data belongs to (e.g., knowing the history of the Titanic). It helps Data Scientists make smart assumptions before looking at the data.
Hypothesis Generation: The process of making educated guesses about your data before analyzing it. (Exactly what you just did!).
Data Leakage / Redundancy: When two features tell you the exact same thing (like your assumption that Fare and Pclass are redundant).
Imputation (Advanced): The process of replacing missing data with substituted values. Instead of global averages, using grouped medians (like median age per Title) drastically reduces error and preserves the data's true distribution.
Feature Mapping / Replacing: The act of standardizing messy text data. (e.g., teaching the computer that "Mlle" and "Ms" mathematically mean the exact same thing as "Miss").
Knowledge #1: Smart Imputation (Feature Extraction): Never fill missing values blindly with a global average (mean/median). Look for hidden "proxy" variables. On the Titanic, extracting "Titles" from the Name column allows us to group passengers. We can then fill a missing "Age" with the median age of that specific title group (e.g., all "Masters" get an age of 4, all "Mrs." get an age of 35).
Knowledge #2: The Golden Rule of Imputation: Always visualize the distribution of a feature before you fill missing values, and after you fill them. If the shape of your chart changes drastically, your imputation method was bad and introduced bias.
Knowledge #3: Binning (Discretization): Turning continuous numbers (like Age 0-80) into distinct categories (Child, Adult, Senior) helps models capture non-linear relationships and ignore minor noise in the data.
Knowledge Entry #1: Missingness is Information. Data isn't always missing by accident. Sometimes the reason it is missing (e.g., a person died and their records were lost) is a powerful predictor. Instead of just erasing the fact that it was missing by filling it, we can create a flag (like Age_Is_Missing = 1) to tell the model, "Hey, this data was unknown, and that might be a clue."
Knowledge Entry #2: Protecting the Model from Over-Memorizing. If we leave rare, unique data (like a one-of-a-kind title "Jonkheer") alone, the model might memorize that specific person's fate instead of learning a broad rule. We group rare things together so the model learns human patterns, not human names.
Knowledge Entry #3: Strategic Guessing (Imputation). We never fill missing information with a blind global guess. We look for hidden clues (like someone's Title) to make a highly educated, specific guess, minimizing how much we distort the truth.
Knowledge Entry #5: "Killing Your Darlings." No matter how clever or logical a hypothesis seems in your head, if the data does not show a strong, undeniable signal, you must be willing to throw the idea away. Keeping useless features just because you spent time making them adds "noise" to the model and makes it worse. "It doesn't hurt to check, but it hurts to keep bad data."
Knowledge Entry #6: The Hierarchy of Missing Data.
If a few rows are missing (like 2 in Embarked): Just fill with the most frequent value.
If a moderate amount is missing (like 177 in Age): Use strategic imputation (like grouping by Title).
If the vast majority is missing (like 687 in Cabin): Drop the column entirely. It's beyond saving.
Knowledge Entry #7: The Limits of Correlation Heatmaps. Heatmaps (specifically Pearson correlation) measure the linear relationship between numbers. Text/Categorical data will be ignored by the heatmap unless you "encode" it (turn the text into numbers, like Female=1, Male=0) first.
Knowledge Entry #8: Outlier Detection (Boxplots). Boxplots are the best tool to see the "spread" of your data. The box shows where the middle 50% of your data lives. Any dots floating way outside the box's "whiskers" are mathematical outliers that might confuse your model.
Knowledge Entry #9: The Problem of Magnitude (Scaling vs. Binning). Machine Learning models are easily tricked by big numbers. If a model sees Fare = 100 and Age = 2, it might think Fare is 50x more important. We solve this by either Scaling (shrinking all numbers to a similar range) or Binning (turning continuous numbers into grouped categories like 0, 1, 2, 3).
Knowledge Entry #10: Feature Consolidation. If two columns represent the exact same concept (like SibSp and Parch both representing "Family Members"), models perform better when you combine them into a single, stronger feature (like FamilySize).
Knowledge Entry #11: The Scaling vs. Binning Tradeoff.
    Binning is great for things with natural "stages" (like Age: Child -> Teen -> Adult) because the exact number doesn't matter as much as the life stage.
    Scaling is better for precise, continuous measurements (like Fare, Weight, or Distance) where the exact gaps between numbers hold valuable information that we don't want to destroy.
Knowledge Entry #12: StandardScaler vs. MinMaxScaler. MinMaxScaler shrinks data strictly between 0 and 1. StandardScaler (Z-score) puts the mean average at 0, meaning below-average values become negative. Both are great, but StandardScaler handles wild outliers a bit better.
Knowledge Entry #13: Data Leakage. The biggest mistake beginners make. Never calculate means, medians, or scalers on the combined train+test data. The model must learn only from the training data, treating the test data as a total mystery.
Knowledge Entry #14: Positive vs. Negative Correlation. A positive correlation (like 0.54) means as Feature A goes up, Feature B goes up. A negative correlation (like -0.34) means as Feature A goes up, Feature B goes down. Both are equally powerful for a model!
Knowledge Entry #15: Multicollinearity. When two features in your dataset are highly correlated with each other (not the target). It means they are sharing redundant information. It's not a dealbreaker, but it's something Senior Data Scientists always keep an eye on.
Knowledge Entry #16: Cross-Validation (CV). Never trust a model that is tested on the exact same data it learned from (it will just memorize the answers!). Cross-validation internally slices your training data into smaller "train/test" splits, proving the model can handle unseen data before you ever touch your final test set.
Knowledge Entry #17: The "Seed" (random_state). Algorithms like Random Forest use randomness. If you run it today, you might get 81%. If you run it tomorrow, 82%. To force the computer to be consistent so we can compare models fairly, we set a random_state (usually 42).
Knowledge Entry #18: The Accuracy Ceiling (Aleatoric Uncertainty). In real-world data, 100% accuracy is impossible because of randomness/luck. If you ever see someone with 100% on the Titanic Kaggle leaderboard, they cheated by finding the original passenger list online. A true, honest model maxes out around 83-84%.
Knowledge Entry #19: Hyperparameters. The "settings" or "dials" of an algorithm that you set before training begins (like the number of neighbors in KNN, or the depth of a Decision Tree).
Knowledge Entry #20: GridSearchCV. A brute-force search technique. You give it a list of hyperparameters, and it runs Cross-Validation on every single combination to find the undisputed mathematically best settings.
Knowledge Entry #23: Parameters vs. Hyperparameters. Parameters are the rules the model learns on its own from the data (like "Women survive"). Hyperparameters are the settings you control before training starts (like "How deep should the decision tree grow?"). Tuning is purely adjusting the Hyperparameters.
Knowledge Entry #24: The Brute-Force Trap. GridSearchCV is perfect for small datasets like Titanic. But on datasets with millions of rows, it will crash your computer. For big data, you must switch to RandomizedSearchCV or Optuna.
Knowledge Entry #27: fit_transform vs transform.
fit means "learn the rules" (like finding the average price).
transform means "apply the rules".
You only ever fit on the Training data. For the Test data, you ONLY transform so the test data is judged by the exact same mathematical scale as the training data.
Knowledge Entry #28: Model Persistence (Saving Models). Traditional Machine Learning models (Scikit-Learn) are saved as .pkl (Pickle) or .joblib files. Deep Learning models (TensorFlow/Keras) are saved as .h5 or .keras files.
Knowledge Entry #29: Object Memory (The Scaler Rule). When you write scaler = StandardScaler(), you are creating an empty "brain." When you fit() it to the training data, the brain remembers the exact mean and variance. If you type scaler = StandardScaler() a second time, you completely erase that brain. You must reuse the exact same object to transform() the test data.
Knowledge Entry #30: The Final Sanity Check. Before feeding data to a model, always check the data types (df.dtypes). Every single column must be an int or a float. If you see an object (text string), the model will crash.
Knowledge Entry #31: Unseen Categorical Variables. When you deploy a model in the real world, it will eventually encounter a category it has never seen before (like the title "Dona"). A robust data pipeline must always have a "catch-all" bucket (like our 'Rare' category) to safely handle these surprises without crashing the whole program.
Knowledge Entry #35: Overfitting to the Validation Set. If you tune your hyperparameters too aggressively using GridSearch, your model will eventually memorize the practice exams and lose its ability to handle completely new, real-world data. Sometimes, a simpler model (or fewer hyperparameters) performs better in the wild.
Knowledge Entry #36: The "Honest" Baseline. In Kaggle, many top scores rely on "data leaks" (like matching last names across train/test sets) or outright cheating. An honest, robust Machine Learning pipeline on raw demographic data usually scores lower than highly engineered "hacks"—but the honest pipeline is what companies actually want to see in an interview.
